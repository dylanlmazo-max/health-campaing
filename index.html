The Science Behind Our Feeds

Three psychological mechanisms that explain how passive scrolling can gently distort our perspective and well-being.

1. The Selection Bias

The Curated Gallery

Rarely do people publish their moments of doubt, exhaustion, or ordinary boredom. Your mind instinctively compares your unedited daily existence with the polished summaries of thousands of strangers, creating an artificial feeling of inadequacy.

Cognitive Distortion

2. Upward Comparison

Conditioned Self-Esteem

Social comparison theory explains our natural drive to evaluate ourselves against peers. On image-centric feeds, comparison becomes relentless and upward, gradually chipping away at body satisfaction and genuine contentment.

Social Evaluation

3. Variable Dopamine Loops

The Slot Machine of Validation

Feeds rely on intermittent variable reward schedules—the very mechanism behind gambling machines. Unpredictable notifications stimulate dopamine surges followed by subtle dips, encouraging habituated checks and a longing for approval.

IN-DEPTH READING LOUNGE

Understanding the Digital Mind

A quiet space to read without distractions. Explore the psychology of social media, digital dysmorphia, and how to build sustainable digital boundaries.

Social Psychology 3 min read

The Architecture of the Highlight Reel: Why We Compare

When Leon Festinger proposed social comparison theory in 1954, human comparison was local and contextualised. Today, algorithms present us with millions of curated highlight reels every minute.

In face-to-face communities, we observe our neighbours during their ordinary days: carrying groceries, looking tired after work, or having bad hair days. This natural balance provides an intuitive baseline of what being human feels like.

On modern feeds, however, that baseline disappears. An asymmetry is created: we possess full, unedited access to our own struggles, mistakes, and awkward mornings, but we only consume the top 1% of everyone else’s polished output. Your brain inevitably processes this skewed sample as reality, prompting feelings of falling behind even when you are progressing splendidly.

Key Takeaway: Remind yourself that you are comparing your behind-the-scenes footage with someone else’s promotional trailer. They are completely different categories.
Body Image 4 min read

Body Image in the Age of Micro-Editing & Digital Filters

How subtle beauty filters, pore-smoothing algorithms, and automatic contouring gently distort our relationship with our natural physical appearance.

Historically, retouched photographs were limited to fashion magazines and billboard advertisements. We knew professional teams, stylists, and lighting designers had spent hours preparing each frame.

Today, smartphone cameras apply subtle skin-smoothing and jawline-narrowing filters by default. When our peers post casual everyday selfies that appear natural yet contain subtle algorithmic modifications, our brains internalise these impossible aesthetics as the new normal. Over time, looking into an ordinary mirror without digital correction can trigger feelings of dysmorphia and disappointment.

Key Takeaway: Skin texture, pores, and natural asymmetries are not imperfections to be smoothed away; they are biological signs of being alive.
Neuroscience 3 min read

The Dopamine Slot Machine: How Infinite Scroll Works

Discover why pull-to-refresh and infinite scrolling feel so irresistibly magnetic, and how variable reward schedules influence human attention.

B.F. Skinner discovered that animals repeat behaviours most obsessively not when they receive rewards every time, but when rewards occur unpredictably. This psychological phenomenon is known as variable ratio reinforcement.

Every time you swipe down on your feed or pull to refresh, your brain does not know whether the next post will be hilarious, dull, inspiring, or controversial. That brief moment of anticipation releases dopamine—the neurotransmitter of desire and pursuit. Social apps are deliberately engineered around this loop to keep you engaged long after your intention has expired.

Key Takeaway: You are not lacking in willpower. You are simply navigating digital environments designed by behavioural scientists to maximise your dwell time.
Mindful Living 4 min read

Reclaiming Digital Peace: Practical Boundaries That Work

You do not need to delete every application to regain your mental clarity. Learn sensible, sustainable micro-habits that protect your well-being.

Going completely 'off-grid' is often unsustainable in a connected world where academic, professional, and social ties live on digital platforms. A healthier approach is establishing intentional friction.

Start with simple physical boundaries: keep your phone off your bedside table at night, allowing your morning brain thirty quiet minutes before absorbing external opinions. Practise unfollowing or muting accounts that trigger quiet pangs of envy or body dissatisfaction—do so without guilt. Your feed should be a curated garden that nourishes you, not an open billboard that exhausts you.

Key Takeaway: Protecting your peace of mind is not avoidance; it is an act of self-respect in an attention-based economy.

LOCAL SUPPORT & CRISIS CARE

Support in Argentina, Chaco & Resistencia

If digital pressure, anxiety, or low mood ever feel overwhelming, reaching out for compassionate, professional guidance is an act of courage.

Immediate Digital Well-being Habits

  • Curate your feed actively: If an account consistently evokes self-doubt, envy, or body dissatisfaction, mute or unfollow it without second thoughts.
  • Screen-free morning cushion: Protect your first 30 minutes awake from notifications to allow your mind to wake up on its own terms.
  • Remember the behind-the-scenes: When a post sparks comparison, remember that you are looking at someone's best angle, not their whole life.
  • In-person grounding: Connect with a friend face to face or engage in an offline hobby where your eyes are not directed at a glass screen.

STUDENT-LED CAMPAIGN • UNNE

Who Is Behind This Movement?

Conceived, researched, and authored by a collective of 4th-year English students from the Languages Department (Departamento de Idiomas) at Universidad Nacional del Nordeste (UNNE) in Resistencia, Chaco.

Language as an Active Medium in Real-World Contexts

For our team of fourth-year English students at the UNNE Languages Department, this initiative is a purposeful way to use language in real-world contexts. It enables us to feed back positively into our own learning through wholesome, constructive initiatives like this one—using English as our active medium to dismantle digital illusions and foster genuine well-being across our student community.

Throughout our senior year, our discussions frequently examined how contemporary digital spaces shape youth self-esteem and emotional pressure in our region. We noticed our peers—and often ourselves—quietly wrestling with the unspoken expectation to project flawless lives, endless discipline, and aesthetic perfection.

By bringing this campaign to life through our English communication, we establish a meaningful bridge between global psychological research and our regional realities in Chaco and Corrientes. Language is at its most transformative when it enriches both our professional path and our community's emotional well-being.

Our Core Pillars

  • Critical Digital Literacy: Dismantling algorithmic illusions through informed psychological evidence and honest conversation.
  • Empathetic English in Action: Using our English communication to connect global insights with local realities in Argentina.
  • Regional Solidarity (Chaco & Corrientes): Ensuring students and young adults in Resistencia know exactly where to find free, compassionate mental health assistance.
